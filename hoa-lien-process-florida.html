HOA Lien Process in Florida

The notice ladder a Florida HOA has to climb — from a late-assessment letter to a recorded claim of lien — under s. 720.3085, Florida Statutes.

It's a two-step ladder, not a single notice

A Florida HOA generally cannot go straight from "assessment is late" to "lien recorded." Section 720.3085, Florida Statutes, builds in a sequence of notices, each with its own required content, delivery method, and waiting period. Skipping a step, or getting the delivery method wrong, is the kind of defect that can make a later lien vulnerable to challenge — separate from whether the debt itself is real.

  1. Notice of Late Assessment — s. 720.3085(3)
    This is the delinquency notice the statute requires before the association may charge attorney fees related to collecting the debt. It has to be delivered before those fees start accruing. Section 720.3085(3)(d), F.S., prescribes a specific statutory form for this notice — a board should compare its draft against the current statutory form text and conform it, rather than freelancing the wording.
  2. 45-day waiting period
    The statute builds in a 45-day period tied to the Notice of Late Assessment before the association's next remedies — including the notice below — come into play. Verify how the current statute measures this period (e.g., from mailing vs. from receipt) before counting down to a deadline.
  3. Notice of Intent to Record a Claim of Lien — s. 720.3085(4)
    This is the demand notice: it gives the owner 45 days from when the notice is provided to pay the total amount due before the association records a claim of lien against the parcel. The statute specifies how this notice must be sent — understood as registered or certified mail, return receipt requested, plus first-class mail, to the parcel address and any other address the association has on file. Verify current statute text on delivery method before sending; getting this wrong can undermine the notice.

What a recorded lien secures

Once a claim of lien is properly recorded, it generally secures the unpaid assessments together with interest, late charges, and the costs and reasonable attorney fees incurred in collection, as permitted by law and the association's governing documents. That is a meaningful escalation from a late-assessment letter, which is exactly why the statute requires the earlier notice steps first — the lien is not supposed to be the association's opening move.

Sequencing matters more than speed

Boards under pressure to collect sometimes want to skip ahead to recording a lien. The safer path — both for enforceability and for the association's relationship with its members — is to complete each notice in order, use the delivery method the statute requires, and keep records showing each step was actually sent. A Notice of Intent to Record a Claim of Lien sent without a prior, properly delivered Notice of Late Assessment skips a step the statute expects to see completed first.
